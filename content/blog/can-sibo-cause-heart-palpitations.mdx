> **Quick answer:** SIBO isn't established as a direct cause of heart palpitations. Digestive illness may contribute indirectly through diarrhea, dehydration, electrolyte changes, poor intake, anemia, or the body's stress response—but I never assume a fluttering or racing heart is “just the gut.”

“My stomach acts up, then my heart starts pounding. Are they connected?”

They may occur together. That isn't the same as proving SIBO caused an abnormal heart rhythm.

Palpitations are an awareness of your heartbeat. People describe pounding, fluttering, racing, skipping, or an extra-hard beat. Sometimes the rhythm is abnormal. Sometimes monitoring shows a normal rhythm that simply feels unusually noticeable.

Either way, I take it seriously enough to ask the heart questions before the gut questions.

## What could connect digestive symptoms and palpitations?

### Diarrhea and dehydration

Frequent loose stool can reduce fluid volume. Dehydration makes the heart work harder and can cause a faster heartbeat, lightheadedness, and weakness. If you're barely eating or drinking because your stomach feels terrible, that effect can build quietly.

### Electrolyte changes

Sodium, potassium, and magnesium help the heart's electrical system work normally. Significant diarrhea, vomiting, restrictive eating, certain medicines, or overusing laxatives can disturb those levels.

I don't recommend guessing with high-dose electrolyte supplements. A blood test is more useful than assuming every palpitation means low magnesium.

### Anemia or nutritional deficiency

More severe or longstanding intestinal overgrowth may contribute to malabsorption. Iron deficiency or vitamin B12 problems can occur for many reasons and may contribute to fatigue, shortness of breath, dizziness, or a racing heart.

That possibility is evaluated with history and laboratory testing—not diagnosed from SIBO symptoms alone.

### A large meal, pressure, and heightened awareness

Some patients notice palpitations after eating, especially when they feel very full or uncomfortable. Meal size, reflux, caffeine, alcohol, blood-sugar shifts, and the autonomic nervous system may all influence what they feel.

I avoid the easy story that trapped gas is physically “pushing on the heart.” That explanation is usually too simple and can distract from a real rhythm problem.

### Stress can amplify both sensations

Digestive symptoms are stressful. Anxiety and adrenaline can increase heart rate and make normal beats more noticeable. That doesn't mean the symptom is imaginary, and it doesn't give us permission to stop evaluating it.

## Common causes I don't want you to miss

Palpitations can come from premature beats, atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias, thyroid disease, anemia, fever, dehydration, sleep loss, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stimulant medicines, decongestants, asthma medicines, or recreational drugs.

SIBO belongs much farther down that list as a possible **indirect contributor**, not a first conclusion.

## How I sort this out

I ask:

- Does the heartbeat feel fast, irregular, forceful, or skipped?
- How long does it last?
- Does it start after meals, standing, exercise, caffeine, or a bowel movement?
- Is there chest pressure, shortness of breath, dizziness, or fainting?
- Are diarrhea, vomiting, weight loss, or restricted eating present?
- Which medicines and supplements are you taking?

Depending on the answers, evaluation may include an ECG, rhythm monitor, blood count, electrolytes, thyroid testing, and other cardiac assessment. Digestive testing comes after immediate heart concerns are addressed.

## When SIBO testing may make sense

If palpitations occur alongside persistent bloating, gas, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal discomfort, and meaningful SIBO risk factors, I may evaluate the digestive pattern separately. A breath test can support SIBO or intestinal methanogen overgrowth when performed and interpreted correctly.

It can't tell me whether your heart rhythm is safe.

## When to get urgent help

Call emergency services for palpitations with chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, fainting or near-fainting, new weakness, sweating, confusion, or a sustained very fast or very slow heart rate—especially if this is new for you.

Don't wait for a digestive appointment and don't drive yourself if you feel faint.

## What you can record before a routine appointment

If symptoms are brief and you have no emergency warning signs, note the time, duration, pulse if available, meal, caffeine or alcohol, body position, digestive symptoms, and medicines taken that day. A smartwatch can be helpful, but it doesn't replace medical rhythm testing.

Bring the pattern. Don't arrive with only the conclusion, “SIBO did it.”

## My bottom line

The gut and cardiovascular system don't live in separate boxes, but a possible connection shouldn't become a shortcut. I check hydration, electrolytes, nutrition, and digestive symptoms while making sure the heart gets its own proper evaluation.

Your gut may be part of the story. I won't assume it's the whole story.
