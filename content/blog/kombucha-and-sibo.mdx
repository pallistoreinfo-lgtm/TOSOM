> **Dr. J’s quick answer:** Kombucha does not treat SIBO, and its sugar, carbonation, acidity, and fermentation products can aggravate symptoms in some people. If you try it, use a small amount and judge it by your response—not the marketing.

If you’re struggling with bloating, cramping, or other gut health problems, there’s a good chance you’re fighting with a condition called SIBO—Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth.

SIBO can be difficult to diagnose and may recur. Kombucha is not an established treatment, but patients often ask whether it is safe to drink and whether its live cultures are helpful.

## **What Is SIBO, and Why Should You Care?**

SIBO occurs when bacteria that should be living in the large intestine begin to take up residence in the small intestine, where they don’t belong. This imbalance in gut bacteria disrupts digestion and nutrient absorption, and causes a long list of symptoms: gas, bloating, constipation or diarrhea, fatigue, skin issues, and even brain fog.

Clinical guidelines discuss antibiotics such as rifaximin for selected patients. Recurrence can occur, so follow-up should consider motility, anatomy, medications, and whether the original diagnosis still fits. That does not make kombucha a substitute for diagnosis or treatment.

That is why kombucha deserves a careful—not promotional—look.

## **Kombucha 101: What Makes It Unique?**

Kombucha is a fermented tea made with green or black tea, sugar, and a SCOBY—a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast. Fermentation changes the sugar and produces organic acids, small amounts of alcohol, carbonation, and a variable mixture of microorganisms. The final sugar, alcohol, and microbial content differs by product and batch.

The final product is a lightly effervescent, tangy beverage that’s been consumed for centuries in Asia and Eastern Europe for its supposed digestive and immune-boosting properties.

## **But How Can It Help Fight SIBO?**

### **1. Laboratory findings are not SIBO treatment evidence**

Kombucha and some of its acids can inhibit microbes in laboratory conditions. That does not show that drinking kombucha safely reduces bacterial overgrowth in the human small intestine. I do not use food-lab antimicrobial findings as proof of clinical treatment, and I do not portray prescribed antibiotics as simply destroying every helpful organism.

### **2\. Restoring Microbial Balance with Probiotics**

Kombucha may contain live organisms, but it is not a standardized probiotic product. A study of a specific probiotic strain cannot be transferred automatically to kombucha, and greater variety in a bottle does not prove that it will improve a person’s intestinal microbiome or prevent SIBO recurrence.

### **3\. Supports Digestive Motility—A Key SIBO Factor**

SIBO often arises from delayed gastrointestinal motility, constipation. Food and bacteria that sit too long in the small intestine give the wrong bacteria a chance to overpopulate.

There is not enough clinical evidence to say kombucha improves human “liver detoxification,” increases digestive enzymes, or normalizes bowel transit. Carbonation may actually worsen belching and distention in sensitive people.

This aligns with the philosophy of natural medicine, which teaches that improving the movement of food through the digestive tract is essential to avoid harmful bacterial overgrowth, which leads to bloating and gas.

## **Isn’t Kombucha a Fermented Sugar Bomb?**

Great question. While kombucha begins with sugar, most of it is broken down during fermentation. Look for store-bought versions that contain between 2 to 6 grams of sugar per 8 oz, depending on how long the brew ferments. My favorite brand is Synergy, sweetened with fruit juice and a minimal amount of sugar.

For those with active SIBO symptoms, sugar, carbonation, acidity, caffeine, and fermentation products may affect tolerance. A low-sugar commercial product may be easier for some people, but it is not proven beneficial during treatment. Home brewing adds contamination and uncontrolled alcohol risks, so I do not recommend it as a medical strategy.

## **Who Should Avoid Kombucha?**

As beneficial as kombucha can be, it’s not for everyone, at least not right away. If you’re in the early, symptomatic stages of SIBO or have histamine intolerance, the natural byproducts of fermentation may worsen your symptoms.

In those cases, it’s wise to start small—try just a 1-2 oz sip and monitor how you feel. Once your gut begins to heal, many people find they tolerate fermented foods much better. ## **Final Thoughts: A Functional Tool in a Bigger Toolbox**

![Kombucha and SIBO](/assets/images/2025/07/Kombucha-and-SIBO-1.jpg)

Kombucha is not a treatment or cure for SIBO. It is an optional beverage that some people tolerate and others do not. If you enjoy it without worsening symptoms, a small serving can fit your diet; if it increases bloating, reflux, pain, or diarrhea, there is no health reason to force it.

## Dr. J’s evidence-based perspective

In my clinical work, I treat symptoms as clues—not proof of a diagnosis. I listen to the full history, look for common and serious alternatives, and use testing when it can change the plan. I also avoid turning a short-term symptom diet into a permanent list of forbidden foods.

## When to seek medical care

Contact a clinician promptly for blood in the stool, black stool, persistent vomiting, fever, dehydration, anemia, progressive trouble swallowing, severe abdominal pain, or unintentional weight loss. These are not symptoms to manage with diet or supplements alone.
